Marine Insurance in India

Marine insurance covers the loss of or damage to goods, vessels and freight while they are being moved — by sea, air, road, rail or inland waterway. For most Indian businesses it means marine cargo insurance: cover for shipments of raw materials and finished goods in domestic and international transit. Ship owners also buy hull and marine liability cover.

In short

  • Four branches: cargo (the goods), hull (the vessel), freight (the carrier's revenue), and marine liability (P&I, charterers).
  • Cargo cover levels: Institute Cargo Clauses (A) all-risk, (B) named perils, (C) major-casualty only.
  • Who insures: depends on the Incoterm — under CIF the seller insures to destination; under FOB the buyer insures.
  • Sum insured is usually invoice (CIF) value plus 10–15% to cover incidental costs and expected profit.
  • Policy types: a specific (single-transit) policy for one shipment, or an open / annual policy that covers all shipments in a period.

What is Marine Insurance in India?

Marine insurance is among the oldest forms of insurance and, in India, is governed by the Marine Insurance Act 1963. It responds when goods or vessels suffer physical loss or damage in transit — a container swept overboard, a truck overturning, water damage in a hold, theft from a warehouse in the course of transit, or a general-average contribution after a maritime incident.

The branch almost every trading, manufacturing or logistics business needs is marine cargo. A ship owner or operator additionally needs hull and machinery cover for the vessel and protection and indemnity (P&I) for third-party liabilities. Coverage

What marine insurance covers — and what it doesn’t

A quick side-by-side so you know exactly where the policy helps.

Typically covered

  • Physical loss of or damage to cargo from fire, sinking, stranding, collision and overturning
  • Jettison, washing overboard and water damage (subject to the clause level chosen)
  • Theft, pilferage and non-delivery of whole packages (ICC A, or as an extension)
  • General average and salvage contributions
  • Loss during loading, unloading and transhipment, and during ordinary storage in transit
  • Hull and machinery damage to an insured vessel (hull policies)
  • Third-party and pollution liabilities of a vessel (P&I / marine liability)

Usually excluded

  • Ordinary leakage, loss in weight or volume, and wear and tear
  • Inherent vice or the nature of the goods (e.g. perishables decaying naturally)
  • Insufficient or unsuitable packing and preparation of the cargo
  • Deliberate misconduct of the insured and delay-related loss (even if caused by a peril)
  • War and strikes unless the war and SRCC clauses are added
  • Unseaworthiness of the vessel where the insured is aware of it
  • Loss where the claim is not notified promptly or the carrier is not held liable

Exact inclusions and exclusions vary by insurer and plan — your BTW IMF advisor walks you through the wording before you buy.

Options

Types of marine insurance

Match the policy to how often you ship and what you move.

Specific (single-transit) cargo policy

Covers one named shipment from origin to destination. Bought per consignment, common for occasional or high-value one-offs.

  • Per-shipment cover
  • Choose clause level per risk
  • No annual commitment

Open / annual cargo policy

A standing policy covering all shipments in a period against an agreed rate; you declare consignments as they move.

  • Automatic cover for every shipment
  • Simple monthly declaration
  • Better rates by volume

Sales turnover policy (STOP)

Premium linked to annual sales turnover rather than per-shipment declarations — minimal paperwork.

  • Cover follows turnover
  • Very low administration
  • Annual adjustment

Hull & machinery / marine liability

Cover for the vessel itself and for a ship operator's third-party and pollution liabilities.

  • Vessel physical damage
  • P&I liability
  • Charterers' cover available

How to choose the right marine insurance

  1. Match the cover to the risk. Work out the worst realistic loss — a long hospital stay, a total-loss claim, an income gap — and buy enough marine insurance to absorb it.
  2. Compare like for like. Premium is only meaningful next to the sum insured, sub-limits, waiting periods and exclusions. We line these up side by side.
  3. Check the insurer, not just the plan. Claim-settlement record, turnaround time and how the insurer treats disclosure disputes matter as much as price.
  4. Add only the riders you'll use. A few add-ons sharply improve real payouts; the rest just raise the premium.

Who needs marine insurance?

Any business whose goods move: importers and exporters, manufacturers dispatching to dealers and customers, distributors, e-commerce and 3PL/logistics companies, and project contractors moving plant and equipment. Under CIF and CIP contracts the seller is contractually obliged to insure. Ship owners, charterers and freight forwarders need the hull and liability branches. Banks financing trade usually insist on marine cover as a condition.

What affects your premium

  • Nature of the goods and their susceptibility to damage, theft or spoilage
  • Mode(s) of transit, route, and countries / ports involved
  • Clause level chosen — ICC (A), (B) or (C) — and whether war/SRCC is added
  • Packing standard and use of containers vs break-bulk
  • Sum insured basis (CIF + 10–15%) and any duty or increased-value cover
  • Your claims history and, for open policies, annual volume

FAQs

Marine Insurance in India — frequently asked questions

What is marine insurance and what does it cover?

Marine insurance covers physical loss of or damage to goods, vessels and freight during transit by sea, air, road, rail or inland waterway. For most businesses this means marine cargo cover for their shipments. Ship owners also buy hull cover for the vessel and P&I cover for third-party liabilities.

Is marine insurance mandatory?

It is not a statutory requirement, but it is contractually required under CIF and CIP sales terms, and banks financing imports or exports almost always insist on it. Given that carriers' liability is capped very low by law, shipping uninsured leaves the goods' owner exposed to the full value of any loss.

Who is responsible for insuring the goods — the buyer or the seller?

It depends on the Incoterm. Under CIF or CIP the seller must arrange insurance to the destination. Under FOB, CFR, EXW and most other terms the risk passes to the buyer at an earlier point, so the buyer should insure. BTW IMF checks your contracts and closes any gap.

What are Institute Cargo Clauses A, B and C?

They are standard international sets of cargo cover. ICC (A) is the widest — 'all risks' of physical loss or damage except listed exclusions. ICC (B) covers a defined list of perils including water damage. ICC (C) is the narrowest, covering only major casualties like fire, sinking, collision and overturning. Most cargo is insured on (A).

What is an open marine policy?

An open (or annual) policy is a standing agreement that automatically covers every shipment you make during the policy period at pre-agreed rates. You simply declare consignments as they move, instead of buying a separate policy each time. It suits any business that ships regularly.

How is the sum insured for a shipment decided?

The market norm is the CIF (cost, insurance, freight) value of the goods plus 10–15%. The uplift covers incidental expenses and the profit you expected to make, so a total loss leaves you whole rather than merely reimbursed for cost.
