Project Insurance

Project Insurance (CAR & EAR)

Project insurance protects a construction or installation project against physical loss or damage while the work is being carried out, and covers third-party injury or damage on the site. Contractors' All Risks (CAR) covers civil construction; Erection All Risks (EAR) covers the installation and testing of plant and machinery. Cover runs for the project period plus a maintenance/defects period.

  • Civil works (CAR) or plant erection (EAR)
  • Covers materials, works & plant on site
  • Third-party liability on site
  • Optional delay-in-start-up cover

In short

  • CAR vs EAR: CAR for buildings, roads, bridges and civil infrastructure; EAR for erecting and commissioning machinery, plant and equipment.
  • Who buys it: the principal (project owner) or the main contractor, usually as a single policy covering all parties — owner, contractors and sub-contractors.
  • Sum insured: the completed contract value (including materials, labour, freight, duties and supplied items), not just the contractor's price.
  • Period: from the start of work (or arrival of materials) to handover, plus a maintenance period (commonly 12 months) for defects.
  • Bundled sections: Section I – material damage to the works; Section II – third-party liability. Add-ons cover surrounding property, design defect and delay.

What is Project Insurance (CAR & EAR)?

During construction or erection, a project is exposed to risks that neither a completed-building policy nor a contractor's general insurance addresses — a partly built structure collapsing, a flood washing out foundations, a crane dropping a load, fire in temporary works, theft of materials from the site, or an accident to installed machinery during testing.

A project (CAR/EAR) policy is a single, project-specific contract that covers all of this for the duration of the works. It is normally taken out in the joint names of the project owner, the main contractor and sub-contractors, so a dispute over fault between them does not delay a claim. Section I indemnifies physical loss or damage to the works, materials and construction plant; Section II covers legal liability to third parties arising from the works. Coverage

What project insurance covers — and what it doesn’t

A quick side-by-side so you know exactly where the policy helps.

Typically covered

  • Physical loss or damage to the permanent and temporary works under construction or erection
  • Fire, flood, storm, earthquake, subsidence, and collapse during the works
  • Faulty workmanship and, with an add-on, damage from a defect in design
  • Theft and burglary of materials and equipment from the site
  • Damage to construction plant, machinery and equipment on site
  • Testing and commissioning risks for installed plant (EAR)
  • Third-party bodily injury and property damage arising from the project (Section II), plus clearance of debris and professional fees

Usually excluded

  • Loss discovered only at inventory-taking, and normal wastage of materials
  • The cost of replacing or rectifying defective material or workmanship itself (only the resulting damage, and only with the DE add-on for design)
  • Wear and tear, corrosion, rust, and gradual deterioration
  • Consequential loss, contractual penalties and liquidated damages (delay cover is a separate add-on)
  • War, nuclear risk, and wilful acts or gross negligence of the insured
  • Vehicles licensed for road use and waterborne craft (insured elsewhere)
  • Loss during a period the policy does not cover, e.g. after the maintenance period ends

Exact inclusions and exclusions vary by insurer and plan — your BTW IMF advisor walks you through the wording before you buy.

Options

Types of project insurance

The policy type follows the nature of the project.

Contractors' All Risks (CAR)

For civil construction — buildings, factories, roads, bridges, pipelines, water and infrastructure works.

  • Covers works, materials, temporary works
  • Site plant included
  • Third-party liability section

Erection All Risks (EAR)

For installing and commissioning plant and machinery — power, process, manufacturing lines, HVAC, turbines.

  • Covers erection and testing risks
  • Suits turnkey supply-and-install
  • Cold and hot testing cover

Single-project policy

One policy for one project, in the joint names of owner, contractor and sub-contractors, for the full project period.

  • All parties on one contract
  • Value = completed contract value
  • Maintenance period included

Annual contractors' policy

A blanket policy covering all of a contractor's smaller projects started in the year below a value threshold.

  • Automatic cover per project
  • Declaration or turnover basis
  • Large projects still placed separately

How to choose the right project insurance

  1. Match the cover to the risk. Work out the worst realistic loss — a long hospital stay, a total-loss claim, an income gap — and buy enough project insurance to absorb it.
  2. Compare like for like. Premium is only meaningful next to the sum insured, sub-limits, waiting periods and exclusions. We line these up side by side.
  3. Check the insurer, not just the plan. Claim-settlement record, turnaround time and how the insurer treats disclosure disputes matter as much as price.
  4. Add only the riders you'll use. A few add-ons sharply improve real payouts; the rest just raise the premium.

Who needs project insurance?

Project owners, developers, EPC and turnkey contractors, civil and MEP sub-contractors, and equipment suppliers with an installation scope. It is standard practice — and usually a tender or lender condition — for any project above a modest value to have a CAR or EAR policy in the joint names of all parties before work starts. Consultants and designers may additionally need professional indemnity.

What affects your premium

  • Completed contract value and the type of project (civil, industrial, infrastructure)
  • Project duration and the maintenance/defects period selected
  • Site conditions — flood plain, seismic zone, monsoon exposure, ground conditions
  • Scope of testing and commissioning (EAR) and any hot testing
  • Add-ons — surrounding property, design defect (DE3/DE4/DE5), delay in start-up, escalation
  • Contractor experience, method statements and risk controls

How a project insurance claim works

A dedicated claim manager prepares the file and follows up with the insurer until it’s settled.

Make safe and notify

Secure the site, prevent further loss, and notify us / the insurer immediately — most policies require notice within 24–48 hours.

Preserve the site for survey

Do not clear or rebuild the damaged section until the surveyor has inspected, except emergency measures to limit damage.

Compile the claim

Progress records, photographs, the bill of quantities, cause report, repair method and cost estimate, and any third-party correspondence.

Settlement

The insurer pays the cost of repairing or rebuilding the damaged works, less the excess and any average adjustment; third-party claims are handled under Section II.

Useful add-ons & riders

Riders widen project insurance for a small extra premium. The ones worth considering:

  • Damage to surrounding / existing property of the principal
  • Design defect cover (DE3, DE4 or DE5 wording)
  • Advanced Loss of Profit / Delay in Start-Up (ALOP/DSU) for the owner
  • Escalation clause for rising material and labour costs
  • Offsite storage and inland transit of major components
  • Extended maintenance / guarantee period cover
FAQs

Project Insurance (CAR & EAR) — frequently asked questions

What is the difference between CAR and EAR insurance?

Contractors' All Risks (CAR) covers civil construction works — buildings, roads, bridges, pipelines and infrastructure. Erection All Risks (EAR) covers the installation, erection and commissioning of plant and machinery, including the risks of testing. A project that is mostly civil with some plant, or vice versa, is placed on the policy matching its dominant nature, with the other element added.

Who should take out the project insurance — the owner or the contractor?

Either can, but it is placed in the joint names of the project owner, the main contractor and all sub-contractors so that a claim is not delayed by arguments over which party was at fault. Larger owners and EPC contracts usually have the principal arrange a single 'principal-controlled' policy for the whole project.

What sum insured should a project policy have?

The completed contract value — the full value of the works when finished, including all materials (even those supplied free by the owner), labour, freight, customs duties and site preparation. Insuring only the contractor's price leaves the project underinsured and every claim reduced by the average clause.

Does project insurance cover delays and penalties?

Not under the standard cover, which pays for physical damage. If an insured event delays the project and the owner loses revenue or incurs penalties, that is covered only by adding an Advanced Loss of Profit / Delay in Start-Up (ALOP/DSU) section, which is underwritten carefully and priced separately.

When does the cover start and end?

Cover starts from the commencement of work at the site, or the unloading of the first materials, whichever is applicable, and continues until the works are handed over or taken into use. It then runs through a maintenance (defects liability) period — commonly 12 months — during which damage caused by the contractor while making good defects is also covered.

Is project insurance mandatory?

There is no general statutory mandate, but it is required in practice: construction and EPC contracts almost always oblige the contractor (or principal) to maintain CAR/EAR cover in joint names, and lenders financing a project make it a disbursement condition. Public liability under the site is also frequently a legal and contractual requirement.
