Critical Illness Insurance

Critical illness insurance pays you a fixed lump sum if you are diagnosed with one of the serious illnesses listed in the policy — commonly cancer, heart attack, stroke, kidney failure and major organ transplant. The money is paid regardless of your actual hospital bills, so you can use it for treatment, to replace lost income, or to clear a loan while you recover.

In short

  • How it pays: a one-time lump sum on first diagnosis of a covered illness that meets the policy definition — not a reimbursement of bills.
  • Survival period: you must typically survive 14–30 days after diagnosis for the claim to be payable.
  • Waiting period: usually 90 days from policy start before any critical-illness claim is allowed.
  • Illness count: plans cover from around 10 to 60+ listed conditions — more is not always better; check the definitions.
  • Not a substitute: you still need a regular health insurance plan for hospital bills.

What is Critical Illness Insurance?

A critical illness (CI) policy is a benefit policy. On diagnosis of a listed illness that meets the exact definition in the wording (for example, a heart attack of specified severity or a cancer of specified stage), the insurer pays the full sum insured as a tax-friendly lump sum. Some plans then end; 'multi-pay' plans continue and can pay again for an unrelated illness.

CI cover exists because a serious illness costs far more than hospital bills alone — months away from work, lifestyle changes, home modifications, a family member giving up income to be a carer. Your health insurance pays the hospital; CI cover replaces the income and absorbs the rest. Coverage

What critical illness cover covers — and what it doesn’t

A quick side-by-side so you know exactly where the policy helps.

Typically covered

  • Cancer of specified severity
  • First heart attack (myocardial infarction) of specified severity
  • Open-chest coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) and other major heart surgery
  • Stroke resulting in permanent symptoms
  • Kidney failure requiring regular dialysis
  • Major organ or bone-marrow transplant
  • Permanent paralysis of limbs, and (on wider plans) 20–60 further listed conditions

Usually excluded

  • Any covered illness diagnosed during the initial waiting period (usually first 90 days)
  • Pre-existing critical illnesses and conditions with prior symptoms or investigations
  • Illness where the insured does not survive the survival period (14–30 days)
  • Conditions that do not meet the specific severity definition in the policy
  • Illness arising from alcohol or drug abuse, self-harm or HIV (except as specifically covered)
  • Congenital conditions and, on many plans, illness within a few years of the policy for certain cancers in situ
  • Claims where a material fact about health or family history was not disclosed

Exact inclusions and exclusions vary by insurer and plan — your BTW IMF advisor walks you through the wording before you buy.

Options

Types of critical illness cover

Choose by whether you want a one-time or repeating payout and how it interacts with your other cover.

Standalone lump-sum (single-pay)

Pays the full sum insured once on first diagnosis, then the policy ends. Simple and the most common.

  • Full sum insured on diagnosis
  • Policy ends after the claim
  • Widest choice of insurers

Multi-pay critical illness

Keeps running after a claim and can pay again for an unrelated illness group, sometimes with an increasing sum insured.

  • Multiple claims possible
  • Illnesses grouped by system
  • Costlier than single-pay

Cancer-specific / heart-specific plan

Focuses on one disease family with staged payouts (early stage, major stage) and premium waiver on early-stage claim.

  • Staged payouts
  • Premium waiver benefit
  • Narrower scope

Critical illness rider

Attached to a term or health policy. Cheapest route to some CI cover, with fewer conditions and lower limits.

  • Low incremental premium
  • Fewer listed illnesses
  • Ends with the base policy

How to choose the right critical illness cover

  1. Match the cover to the risk. Work out the worst realistic loss — a long hospital stay, a total-loss claim, an income gap — and buy enough critical illness cover to absorb it.
  2. Compare like for like. Premium is only meaningful next to the sum insured, sub-limits, waiting periods and exclusions. We line these up side by side.
  3. Check the insurer, not just the plan. Claim-settlement record, turnaround time and how the insurer treats disclosure disputes matter as much as price.
  4. Add only the riders you'll use. A few add-ons sharply improve real payouts; the rest just raise the premium.

Who needs critical illness cover?

Anyone whose household would struggle if their income stopped for a year — primary earners, the self-employed, and people with a home or business loan. It matters even more if you have a family history of cancer, heart disease, diabetes-related kidney disease or stroke. It is a complement to, not a replacement for, a comprehensive health plan.

What affects your premium

  • Age at entry and policy term
  • Sum insured chosen (commonly ₹10–50 lakh, or 3–5 times annual income)
  • Number of illnesses covered and single-pay vs multi-pay
  • Smoking/tobacco use and BMI
  • Personal and family medical history
  • Whether bought as a standalone plan or a rider

FAQs

Critical Illness Insurance — frequently asked questions

Do I need critical illness insurance if I already have health insurance?

Yes, they do different jobs. Health insurance reimburses hospital bills. Critical illness insurance pays a lump sum you can use for anything — replacing lost income during a long recovery, paying for a carer, clearing a loan, or covering costs the hospital bill doesn't. Most financial plans include both.

What illnesses does critical illness insurance cover?

Core plans cover a defined list, usually including cancer of specified severity, first heart attack, major heart surgery, stroke with lasting effects, kidney failure needing dialysis, major organ transplant and permanent paralysis. Wider plans list 20 to 60+ conditions. The precise medical definition of each illness matters more than the number.

What is the survival period in a critical illness policy?

It is the minimum number of days (typically 14 to 30) you must survive after being diagnosed with a covered illness for the claim to be payable. If death occurs within this window, the critical illness benefit is not paid, though a life policy would respond.

When can I claim after buying the policy?

There is an initial waiting period, usually 90 days, during which no critical illness claim is admissible. A condition first diagnosed in this period is excluded even if treatment continues afterwards.

Is the critical illness payout taxable?

The lump sum is generally received tax-free. Premiums paid for a critical illness health policy usually qualify for the Section 80D deduction; premiums for a CI rider on a life policy fall under the life-insurance rules.

Single-pay or multi-pay — which should I choose?

Single-pay (pays once, then ends) is cheaper and sufficient for most people, whose main goal is to bridge one income shock. Multi-pay costs more but keeps paying for unrelated illnesses and suits those wanting decades of protection or with a strong family history of multiple conditions.
